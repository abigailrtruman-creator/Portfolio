Overview

PwC Deals practitioners in more than 60 territories used a cloud platform to turn client data into the analysis behind financial due diligence. It handled the basics of building a data model, but at the most important step, practitioners still left it for Excel.

As lead designer, I led the research, set the design direction and designed 10 new features for the platform. This case study follows the one that closed that gap: the DimMetric Editor, which brought practitioners back from Excel.

Who I led the work with

Product owners
I guided their prioritisation, so the MVP they committed to was built around what practitioners needed most.
Tech
I worked through their proof of concept with them, so gaps surfaced early, before they became build problems.
The business and super users
I brought them in to stress-test the design, surfacing edge cases and territory-specific needs while they were still cheap to fix.
Practitioners
I shadowed them at work to see where mapping really happened, then tested the design with eight across four countries, so it was shaped by how they actually work.

01

Finding the step that kept practitioners in Excel

The platform combined data well, but it couldn’t define hierarchies, and financial statements are built on them. So at the most important step, practitioners left the tool to map accounts by hand in Excel.

I spent a day shadowing Deals practitioners and interviewing them informally, to see where mapping happened, how it varied from person to person, and where the time went.

The cost wasn’t only time. Because every team mapped by hand in its own way, the platform couldn’t deliver the one thing it promised, a consistent approach to due diligence, at the step where it mattered most.

BeforeMapped by hand in Excel
Practitioners left the platform to build financial hierarchies manually. Slow, and done differently by every team.
AfterBuilt in the platform
Practitioners create and manage hierarchies in the tool, with accounts tagged to the right data points the same way everywhere.

The goalPractitioners should be able to prepare their financial data from start to finish without leaving the tool.

02

Getting product, tech and the business to one answer

Product owned the roadmap, tech owned what was feasible, and the business owned the financial logic. The feature only worked if all three agreed on what it had to do.

Turning research into a scope everyone signed up to.

I ran a workshop with the product owners to turn what I’d learnt into a full set of requirements, then took them to the tech team to test against their proof of concept, so we found the gaps before build rather than during it.

There was more than one release’s worth of work, so I led a prioritisation of every requirement by technical effort and user impact. It gave product and tech one shared view of what mattered most, and it became the agreed scope for the MVP.

Getting the financial logic exactly right.

Financial hierarchies follow strict rules. If the editor felt easy but broke them, practitioners would stop trusting it and go back to Excel. I ran working sessions with product, tech and business stakeholders to co-define the target journey, and checked every rule with the product owner against how Deals defines hierarchies.

Six decisions in the editor.

After several rounds of exploration and feedback, I designed the DimMetric Editor within the existing design system, so it felt native to the platform, and built a full prototype in Figma to test the whole journey. It’s the core screen of data modelling: where practitioners build financial hierarchies and assign accounts to each level.

  1. 01

    Three tabs for three parts of the job

    • Mapping, Flags and Sort each get their own tab
    • Optional steps stay out of the way until needed
  2. 02

    Two ways to bring in a hierarchy

    • Upload your own master mapping, or start from a template
    • Supports both standard and custom hierarchies
  3. 03

    One place to change the view

    • Columns, statement and version, all above the table
    • Change what you see without leaving it
  4. 04

    Tags for the lowest level

    • Lowest-level accounts get their own style
    • See what’s linked at a glance in a long table
  5. 05

    Earlier mappings alongside

    • Previous versions sit beside the new mapping
    • Added after testing: practitioners map more than once
  6. 06

    Progress you can see

    • Assign selected accounts from a floating action bar
    • Counters show what’s left and where each account sits

03

Proving it with practitioners across four countries

A tool used in 60+ territories can’t be validated in one office. Testing had to show whether the feature worked for practitioners whose processes, language and experience differed.

Research built around the hard part.

Before formal testing, I ran light-touch sessions with super users to catch territory-specific needs early. The hierarchy logic was the hardest part of the feature, so I led the research and wrote the discussion guide myself to probe how practitioners reasoned through it, as well as whether they could find their way.

I built the guide around three things the design had to get right:

Do the two ways in make sense?
Master mapping and Hierarchy template had to read as two different starting points before anyone clicked.“Could you describe the differences between ‘Master Mapping’ and ‘Hierarchy Template’, based on what you see?”
Does the language travel?
Terms like idMetric and L1–L6 made sense to one team. They had to make sense in every territory.“What is your understanding of ‘idMetric’ and ‘L1–L6’?”
Can people find their own way?
Practitioners had to spot how to edit a mapping from the buttons and links on the page, on their own.“From this page, if you wanted to edit your account mapping table, what would you do?”

We tested with eight practitioners in the UK, India, Germany and the US, both new and experienced users of the platform.

Insight

Practitioners knew their process. What they lacked was confidence in a new interface.

What changed.

The design was well received. Where practitioners hesitated, it was rarely about the finance; it was about the screen. They weren’t sure where to begin, they wanted to check new work against what they’d mapped before, and they needed to see the result in the shape they knew. I made one change for each.

  • Where to start. I’d designed a clean, minimal interface, but it overwhelmed many practitioners at first. I changed course and added on-screen guidance that shows where to begin.

  • Something to check against. Practitioners map more than once, so their earlier mappings now sit beside the new one, as a reference.

  • The shape they know. Collapsible levels in the preview let them fold the hierarchy down to the core financials and check it at a glance.

Several smaller usability fixes went in alongside them.

What I’d tell another designer.

In complex tools, confidence matters more than polish.
I started minimal and testing showed me that practitioners needed guidance more than a clean screen. Guidance is what gave them the confidence to use it.
Name things in words every territory uses.
Feature names that made sense to one team confused another. Switching to terms everyone understood made a visible difference to adoption across territories.
Keep test prototypes simple.
Figma variables and variants made the prototype slow, which threw users off in testing. Going back to separate screens for each state kept the sessions focused on the design.
