Overview

I joined Junction, PwC’s client delivery platform for Deals, after the core product had already been built. Historically practitioners handed clients their analysis as 200-page reports, burdensome at both ends. Junction replaced that with something continuous: less project management overhead, better collaboration, and expert insight reaching clients early enough to shorten their decision-making. My role was to assess the experience as it stood, find where it was costing people time, and deliver the improvements that came out of that.

On the surface, this was feature work on a platform that already worked. Underneath, it was an alignment problem. Junction did not operate alone: territories had built their own tools around it, so a decision about something as ordinary as who can see what needed agreement from senior stakeholders across regions who each wanted a different end state. What follows is one feature and one research study, as the example.

01

A platform already live, and a seam around it.

No blank page here and no rebuild. Junction was built, shipped and in use, which made the work a different shape: finding where a product people already depended on was quietly costing them time, and fixing it without disrupting the engagements running on it.

When a Deals engagement begins there are numerous workspaces to stand up before any work can be delivered, and each one wants much the same information: users, roles, client, project type. Several territories had solved that locally, building their own provisioning tools so that one project initiation journey could set all of them up at once.

02

Roles that didn’t travel between the tools.

Research surfaced user management between the platforms as the pain point. Roles didn’t mean the same thing in each tool, and a permission changed in one didn’t reliably reach the others. People ended up holding access they shouldn’t have had, or without access they had been given.

Individually these were small errors. Together they did something worse than waste time: they cost people confidence in the tools, which took the value straight back out of provisioning everything in one pass. I was asked to help the business align on a strategy, then design the solution across the connected platforms.

03

Three options, priced. Then the deadline picked one.

Alignment was the hard part. The decision landed on multiple territories and multiple groups of users, which meant agreement from several senior stakeholders who each held a different vision of where this should end up.

I mapped the feasible options and set out how roles and permissions would flow between the tools under each one, then made balanced recommendations rather than arguing for a favourite: what each option bought the user, what it cost to build, and what it was genuinely worth to the business.

The client was under pressure to move fast and rebuild confidence across territories, so the quick win won. Users could configure roles as they needed on a platform that was stable and reliable, and the business logic underneath stayed more complicated than it should have been. It was the right call for that moment. It was also a debt, and quick wins of this kind get repaid later at a higher rate.

04

Designing around logic I couldn’t change.

With the deadline set, the job was to build an experience that either kept the agreed logic out of the user’s way or signposted it so plainly it stopped being a trap. Working from the design system, I designed each affected journey and set the responsiveness guidance for the agreed breakpoints: scrollable tabs on narrow screens, a more compact user card. Then iterated with design leadership and the client through to sign-off.

Users were sectioned by the role they held, which made the page scannable and let someone be added straight into a role rather than added and then assigned. Alongside it I explored a master table of every user, with filtering, conditional logic, global search and a single point of action for adding someone. And where the logic was genuinely confusing, large, unmissable banners said so and named the platform or process that actually handled it.

05

The mechanics that decide whether a page is quick.

Accepting a new user took one click, but an admin was rarely accepting one. A multi-select with a global checkbox at the top meant selecting everybody and deselecting the few exceptions, so a large intake was approved in one pass rather than forty. Switching someone’s role moved into the more menu on their row; before that, changing a role meant deleting the user and adding them back, a destructive operation standing in for a routine one.

What lands afterwards matters as much. An approval arrives with a success toast for whoever made it and a ‘new’ tag beside the name for everybody else: the practitioners who didn’t make the change and would otherwise have no way of noticing it. The tag lasts up to a week and clears once someone has opened the page and left again, because acknowledged is a better trigger than expired.

06

The week in Tampa that reopened it.

Quick wins need investment later, and sometimes that means undoing what shipped. Armed with user feedback and the case for the better experience, we reopened the strategy during a week of planning with core leadership in Tampa: the pain points, the opportunities to improve, and several routes out of it.

Being physically in the room was the whole difference. I documented each approach as it was discussed so there was always something to point at, and business directors started sketching their own ideas on top of it. By the end of the week, addressing this properly was agreed as a priority.

07

The feature nobody will ever praise.

Deployed, this let thousands of users manage their project participants and clients with confidence: permissions assigned correctly, and terminology that meant the same thing whichever region you were working in.

There are no metrics to put against it, and there shouldn’t be. User management is supposed to disappear. Get it right and people go back to thinking about the engagement instead of the software.

08

Does the report builder actually save anyone time?

The report builder takes everything uploaded to Junction (content, data, analysis) and automates a fully formatted report ready to share with a client. A round of design iterations had added several things to it: more than one way to organise the content, the option to organise from the table of contents rather than only by sliders, one action to add and remove dividers, report templates, and editable table of contents titles. All of it needed usability testing before it went further.

I ran semi-structured interviews for the context: how a report actually gets made in PowerPoint today, how much of it is customised, whether templates are used at all. Then participants worked through an interactive prototype covering adding and removing sections, customising titles, and organising content.

09

Turning verbatim into something you can show.

I verified every note against the recordings, then mapped all the responses against each question across the topics before categorising the verbatim answers into themes.

This is the analysis. It is the half that earns the recommendations. Done properly, a trend becomes something you can point at rather than something you remember hearing. That matters when the person you are showing it to was not in any of the sessions.

10

Findings, and what they changed.

The report set out the as-is process for creating a report, the pain points inside it, and the usability improvements to make, including the ones that were uncomfortable. Most participants failed to find the call to action the whole feature turns on.

In the playback with the designers and product owners we went through the findings in detail, refined the recommendations together, and agreed the specific actions we would take.

Reflection

What I’m taking from this one.

Three things, and the first is the one I argue about most.

Stakeholders reach for the quick win because it hits a target now, and sometimes that instinct is right. It puts something in front of users and buys real feedback. But on a feature people have no choice but to use, the quick win is a loan. The thorough version is harder to build and it is the one that keeps people on the platform, which is what keeps the business funding it. Making that case early, with the cost of the alternative attached, is part of the job rather than something to raise afterwards.

Managing several senior stakeholders is mostly a scheduling problem wearing a strategy problem’s clothes. Relaying one director’s vision to another in separate rooms isn’t alignment, and defending an idea in siloes is slower than waiting a week to get everyone into one meeting. Tampa is the evidence: a week in the same room moved something that separate conversations hadn’t.

And it is fine to prioritise a research sample. Our users spanned territories and business units with genuinely different needs, and there was never resource to test with all of them. Choosing the groups most likely to adopt first, then phasing a wider sample in behind them, isn’t a compromised study. It’s the study that actually happens.
