Leading discovery on a developer portal for a wholesale full-fibre provider, and mapping the three connected experiences it belonged inside.

  • UX & UI
  • Strategy
  • Content Strategy
Overview

I led discovery on a developer portal for a market disruptor in wholesale full fibre: a hub where developers could find everything they needed to integrate with the required APIs. The contract covered defining the experience and its requirements, aligning with the technical team, and producing initial design templates a later detailed design phase could refine.

On the surface this was one portal. Underneath it was three connected experiences nobody had yet drawn as a single thing: a marketing site to advertise the offering and generate leads, the developer portal we were contracted to build, and a partner portal for organisations to manage their users, permissions, payments and services. Most of what discovery produced came out of treating those as one journey rather than three briefs.

01 / 09 Brief

A hub for developers, and a hint that it wasn’t the whole job.

Our client sells wholesale full fibre, and what they wanted was a developer portal: one place a developer could go for everything needed to integrate with their APIs successfully. Alongside that, they asked us to consider how the portal would fit into something broader — a marketing website on one side, a partner portal on the other.

We were contracted for the initial discovery. Define the experience and the requirements, align with the technical team, and produce initial design templates solid enough to be picked up and refined in a later detailed design phase.

02 / 09 Constraint

Two designers, and a stakeholder with almost no diary.

The design team was two people: me and the head of UX. The client, meanwhile, was going through significant organisational change, and the product stakeholder they had appointed was a C-suite executive with very limited availability.

None of that is incidental, and it is the reason everything below took the shape it did. A small team, an organisation in motion, and a decision-maker we could not simply book whenever a question came up. The process had to produce alignment inside the few sessions we were actually going to get, which meant never arriving at one with an open question where a drafted answer would do.

03 / 09 Alignment

Timelines confirmed, feature list unclear.

The requirements we were handed were broad and the feature list was vague — but the timelines and the resources had already been confirmed. That is the wrong way round, and it is common enough that the first job was simply to make the scope real.

We ran a workshop with the key stakeholders to define the product vision and agree a feature list. With the vision clear, we identified the features and prioritised them into three groups.

Minimum viable product
The essentials. Enough for the platform to be functional and to stand on its own.
Next phase
Prioritised straight after the MVP: significant added value, and achievable in the near term.
Backlog
Either not an immediate priority, or impactful but complex. Revisited against future needs and resources.

We came out of it with a value proposition and a prioritised feature list. Set against the client’s own vision, that was a clear enough path forward to get the work moving.

04 / 09 Requirements

Arriving with a draft, not a list of questions.

A prioritised feature list is not a specification, and refining the requirements underneath it ran straight back into the constraints: a two-person team, a release date the client was ambitious about, and a stakeholder we could not iterate with slowly.

So I reviewed best-in-class examples and wrote the high-level requirements as user stories grounded in that practice, then took them to the client to be validated and iterated on together. Presenting a considered draft turned the few sessions we had from elicitation into decisions, and we aligned on high-level requirements that way.

05 / 09 Reframe

Three experiences, not one portal.

Plenty of questions were still unanswered and a great deal was still happening for the client. Rather than press for answers that were not going to arrive on our timeline, we shifted to helping them define the overarching product strategy — and that is where the shape of the thing changed.

A marketing site to advertise the offering and generate new leads. The developer portal we were already contracted to build. And a partner portal, where organisations could manage their users, permissions, payments and services, and see updates on order and service statistics.

To bring that connected journey to life I built a journey map covering both the developer and the partner experience. It set out the key touchpoints, the places the two overlapped, and the content types each stage depended on. That last part mattered more than it sounds: the client had no content yet and no content strategist, so naming content as part of the journey was the only way it was going to become anyone’s job.

06 / 09 Structure

A site map, to show how much content this really needed.

A developer portal leans almost entirely on content, and at this stage there was none of it. To help the client see the volume involved and where it would all have to sit, I drafted a site map that put features and content pages into one structure.

It did a second job at the same time. Laying the pages out is what identified which ones would need design work, and that list is what the following phase could be scoped against.

07 / 09 Content

Every piece of content, and what it was there to do.

To formalise the content requirements and make sure the coverage was complete, I built a detailed spreadsheet mapping content against the features and pages it belonged to. Against each type it set out guidance on format and style, and the categorisations that would decide how it got handled.

It separated timely from evergreen, so the ongoing effort was visible up front rather than discovered a year later. It carried metrics, so effectiveness could be judged rather than assumed. And it recorded what each piece was for — supporting a sale, deepening engagement, or teaching someone something.

For a client with no content function, that did more than set expectations. It turned content creation into something that could be planned, resourced and handed to somebody.

08 / 09 Templates

Templates that carry their own reasoning.

With the end of the phase in sight, what we owed were wireframes and design templates good enough to be iterated on in detailed design. A wireframe on its own does not survive that handover — whoever picks it up inherits the picture and none of the thinking behind it.

So each template I set up carried five things.

Core wireframe
The essential layout for that feature, which is also what makes the feature’s scope visible.
Feature definition
The scope and functionality, written down so every stakeholder is holding the same version of it.
User stories
The stories we had agreed, kept beside the design so it stays answerable to them.
Content inputs
A summary of the content the feature needs, to guide who writes it and where it lands.
Key considerations
The main open questions still to be resolved before detailed design.

The last of those is the one I would defend hardest. A discovery ends with things unresolved; writing them into the deliverable is the difference between a known gap and a thing quietly overlooked.

09 / 09 Pause

Where it stopped.

Structural changes on the client side paused the work once the discovery phase was complete. The detailed design phase the templates were built for did not follow.

So there is no launch here and no numbers. What there is, is the discovery: a prioritised feature list with a vision behind it, high-level requirements agreed as user stories, a journey map across three experiences, a site map, a content specification, and a set of templates carrying their own open questions.

Reflection

What a discovery that paused still taught me.

Two things I’m taking forward.

The first is that on this project the process was the deliverable. With two designers, an organisation mid-restructure and a stakeholder we could rarely get into a room, what made progress possible was never asking an open question where a drafted answer would do. Best-practice user stories, a journey map, a site map, a content spreadsheet — each one turned a session that would have been elicitation into a session that was a decision. I’d use that sequence again on any engagement where access is the scarce resource.

The second is the one I’d change. We were contracted to define a developer portal, and a good part of the phase went on arguing — correctly — that it could not be defined on its own. The reframe was right. It also arrived late enough that the strategy work had to be fitted around a scope written for something narrower, which is a harder conversation than it needed to be. I’d rather test the edges of a brief at the start of an engagement than in the middle of one.
