Case study · Boots website

Modernising the Boots.com checkout and ‘last mile’ experience that serves 21m customers annually.

  • UX & UI
  • Research
  • Strategy
Overview

As lead product designer, I owned the end-to-end redesign of Boots.com checkout. It serves 21 million UK customers a year. The experience needed modernising, and every step from the first tap to confirmation needed rethinking at the interaction level. Boots was also launching Same-Day Click & Collect, a new proposition built on new technology. It had to sit inside checkout, not beside it. The rebuild goes live in phases from Q4.

Checkout touches almost every part of the business. Marketing, loyalty, personalisation, commerce, payments and fraud each had a stake, and each pulled the product strategy its own way. I held those goals in one view and kept research at the centre of the decisions. Where teams disagreed, how customers actually shop made the call. It had to work for Boots and for its customers, down to the edge cases nobody had mapped.

01 / 10 State

A checkout doing its job, but showing its age.

Boots.com’s legacy checkout worked, but it worked the way flows built five years ago tend to. Customers moved through multiple steps, guided by a progress bar that added friction instead of reducing it. Same-Day Click & Collect, a service the business was investing in heavily, had no place in the flow at all. At 21 million customers a year, every point of friction is multiplied.

The brief was to reshape it. The UI was the easier half. The harder question was whether the flow itself was right.

Before designing anything, I ran an evaluation of the existing experience to pin down what needed to change and why. When product shared the PRD, I tested it against those findings. It had gaps. Rather than design around them, I took them to the product owners and used them to agree where to focus first. The same exercise showed where key decisions sat outside product. That was the point I brought in specialist teams, from payments and fraud through to loyalty and marketing, so their constraints shaped the design from the start instead of surfacing at sign-off.

02 / 10 Insight

Friction isn’t the enemy. Perceived friction is.

Usability testing and previous optimisation tests, backed by a competitive audit across UK retail, kept pointing to the same insight. Customers didn’t struggle with the number of steps. They struggled when they couldn’t see what they’d already entered or where they were going next. The progress bar made it worse. It implied a fixed, linear path that the flow didn’t actually follow.

That set the focus. Surface what customers had already told us, and let them move through checkout more cleanly.

03 / 10 Principle

Same page. No progress bar.

Checkout stayed on a single canvas, with each section unlocking once the one before it was complete. Contact details come first, then delivery and payment. What changed was how the page showed people where they were.

We removed the progress bar but kept the sense of progression. Numbered sections now carry it. With every section collapsed, the whole checkout fits in one viewport, so customers can see from the start that there are only three steps. It sets an expectation of speed before they’ve entered anything.

Progressive disclosure keeps attention on one section at a time. Once complete, each section collapses into a summary customers can check and edit without feeling sent backwards.

Tested against a multi-step alternative, this approach won. Not because it was shorter, but because customers always knew where they stood. Rounds of usability testing, from early concepts through to high-fidelity prototypes, then refined how each section behaved.

04 / 10 Craft

Making the invisible visible.

On mobile and tablet, the payment summary sits in an accordion at the top of the page. Customers have to expand it to see their basket total or the Advantage Card points they’d earn, at the exact moment they’re being asked to hand over money. The rebuild brings it forward. Trust is built or lost in exactly these moments.

05 / 10 Component

A component that outgrew its brief.

The address finder, built on a GBG Loqate integration, was taken on as the address entry pattern for the whole of Boots.com. That wasn’t the goal, but it’s a reminder that a component built well for one flow can save downstream work for years.

06 / 10 Commerce

Bringing loyalty to the surface.

In the old checkout, paying with points was a small grey link in the corner of the payment section. If you didn’t have enough, you only found out by hovering over it.

Moving Advantage Card points into the main list of payment methods gave them the same standing as Apple Pay, card, PayPal and Klarna. Customers can see their balance straight away and whether it covers the full order. That matters more than it sounds. Points have to cover delivery as well, and delivery is only added at checkout, so this is the first moment customers can know for sure. If the balance falls short, the option stays on screen with the amount still showing.

For Advantage Card members, this is the one point in the journey where their points are worth something. Showing that clearly is good for them and good for Boots. With close to 16 million active members, it’s one of the most visible places the business can show customers what their loyalty gets them.

07 / 10 Parity

The confirmation, mirrored.

The order confirmation page uses the same patterns I designed for Boots’ transactional emails. Same layout, same visual language. When the email arrives 30 seconds later, it feels like a continuation of the page the customer has just seen, with everything where they’d expect it to be.

08 / 10 Constraint

When the tech can’t do what customers expect.

Same-Day Click & Collect is the part of this project that didn’t go into build in the shape I would have chosen.

Three integration paths were explored for Availability-to-Promise: OCI out of the box, extending Salesforce, or integrating Blue Yonder. Cost ruled out Blue Yonder, and extending Salesforce didn’t deliver. That left OCI out of the box, a decision taken at leadership level rather than by design.

Generative research showed customers expected same-day collection to work the way it does with the big online retailers they already use. They wanted to see it on the product listing page and have it confirmed in the basket. Most of all, they expected it to hold at checkout. OCI out of the box couldn’t meet those expectations.

The decision wasn’t mine to make, but I made sure it was an informed one. I put together a presentation for the programme leadership team at exec level, setting out what each option would mean for customers and where each one fell short. The platform choice stood. But leadership went into it knowing exactly what customers would and wouldn’t get.

09 / 10 Trade-off

The design that didn’t make the build.

We designed around the constraint anyway. The mixed-availability flow, some items same-day, others not, went through multiple explorations. We landed on the option that caused least disruption: telling customers some items were available today, and offering the soonest day the whole basket would be ready as an alternative.

Later, we discovered even that wasn’t technically feasible. So the build does less. When mixed-availability applies, we say nothing. The alternative is a notification we can’t actually build. I don’t love that. But shipping a lie is worse than shipping less.

10 / 10 Status

Not live yet. Going live in Q4.

The checkout redesign is one part of a multi-year, multi-million pound transformation across Boots. It rolls out in phases from Q4 2026, reaching 21 million UK customers.

Beyond checkout itself, the work has seeded patterns now being taken up across Boots.com. The Loqate address finder and the loyalty component are both being reused elsewhere on the site. The visual language from the transactional emails is spreading too.

Reflection

What I took away from leading this.

Two things I’ll take into the next one.

The screens were the straightforward part. Most of the job was keeping the reasoning intact while product, engineering, research and the business each pulled it somewhere different, and re-making the same argument every time someone new arrived.

The other one is sharper, and it’s the part I’d change. The integration path for Availability-to-Promise was settled before design had scoped what it could do. Everything after that ran inside a constraint that was already fixed: the research showing customers expected same-day collection to work the way it does elsewhere, the mixed-availability flow we designed around it, and the version we couldn’t build. Better screens were never going to move that. Being in the room for the first decision would have.
