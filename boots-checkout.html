Modernising the Boots.com checkout and ‘last mile’ experience that will serve twenty-one million customers annually.

Overview

As lead product designer on the Boots.com checkout rebuild, I led the design of everything from the moment a customer reaches checkout to the last email about their order, for a site that serves 21 million customers a year. That included bringing Same-Day Click & Collect into checkout for the first time, and creating a modular email component system that now powers more than a hundred transactional emails.

I worked closely with product, who owned the requirements, and engineering, who built it on a new platform. Many other teams had a stake in checkout too, from loyalty and marketing to legal. I brought each of them in at the right moments, so their expertise could help shape the design and they could stand behind the result.

The challenge

The brief was to bring Same-Day Click & Collect, a new proposition, into checkout. I saw a bigger opportunity to raise the bar on the whole experience and drive conversion, and made the business case for exactly that. That turned one brief into three connected pieces of work:

01

Proving the case for change in a checkout that already converted

The legacy checkout had a number of things that needed to change. One of the biggest was its structure: one page made to look like five, with a progress bar suggesting steps that didn’t exist. But when that progress bar was introduced, conversion went up. Pointing out problems wasn’t enough. In a checkout that had been optimised for years, any redesign needed a case the data could support, starting with what the existing patterns were actually doing for customers.

01

Proving the case for change in a checkout that already converted

Finding out what the current checkout was really doing.

First, I needed to understand the existing checkout. I ran usability testing on it, plus a study focused on the progress bar and its steps. Customers were split on whether they liked steps at all, but agreed on two things: checkout should feel quick, and it should be easy to go back and change what they’d entered.

So I compared two directions. The first was a true multi-step checkout, with a separate page for each step, which is what a progress bar normally implies. The second kept everything on one page, with numbered sections that collapse into an editable summary once complete.

Testing showed a clear preference for the single page. With every section collapsed, the whole checkout fitted on one screen, so customers could see at a glance how much was left. The numbered sections did the job the progress bar had been doing, without suggesting pages that didn’t exist.

That gave me strong qualitative support. But a checkout that was already converting needed proof in real sales. I took the design to Boots’ optimisation team, a mix of engineers and dedicated designers, who ran it as an A/B test against the live site, with around a million sessions on each version. Conversion rose by 0.78%. Small on paper, but at that scale it meant thousands of extra orders during the test alone, and it was large enough to rule out chance. It settled the argument for change, and gave me a proven foundation to bring Same-Day Click & Collect into.

Beyond the structure, working through the detail with each owner.

The progress bar wasn’t the only thing that needed to change. Other parts of the existing checkout weren’t right either, and I could see it. But seeing it wasn’t enough. Each of those areas was owned by a team with business reasons for how it worked, from commercial partnerships to loyalty strategy. Showing that customers preferred a change counts for little if the business can’t accept it. So I worked through each one with its owner first, and only took a direction into testing once they’d approved it.

Payment methods.
Data showed which methods customers used most, but they had to stay in the order each provider joined Boots, a commercial agreement. So I kept the order and changed the presentation. Every method now sits in one view with equal weight, and choosing card defaults to a saved card, keeping checkout quick while giving every provider equal footing.
Advantage Card points.
Paying with points was a small grey link in a corner, and customers only found out they were short by hovering over it. I moved points into the main payment list with the balance up front. On mobile and tablet, I also brought the order total and points earned out of a collapsed accordion, so customers saw both at the moment they paid.
Marketing consent.
The smallest change took the most convincing. The checkbox sat in the first step, with long and sometimes inaccurate wording. Marketing were understandably risk-averse, so I took them through it line by line: what each phrase was meant to do versus what customers took from it. Once legal signed off new language, marketing agreed to clearer wording and a move to sit beside the terms at payment, where customers expect to check what they’re agreeing to.

Getting each direction approved first meant the usability testing that followed was a much better use of time. It wasn’t spent testing ideas the business might reject. With the owners already behind each change, I led the testing to refine the experience for customers. By the time these changes reached the build, they had the backing of the business and evidence that they made a better experience for customers.

02

Introducing Same-Day Click & Collect, a new proposition with big technical limitations

Mapping the scenarios, and the people behind them.

With the technology undecided, I mapped what the design would have to handle. Retail, pharmacy, dropship and marketplace each affect same-day collection differently, one basket can mix them, and all of it depends on local stock. Behind each basket is a different mindset: someone who urgently needs medicine, and someone buying shampoo who’d happily wait until tomorrow.

I then designed the ideal journey, with availability shown from the product listing page through to checkout. Most of those pages belonged to teams delivering at pace, so I gave each an early heads-up on what their area would need to support.

Making sure leadership chose with the full picture.

The ideal journey depended on true Availability-to-Promise, a multi-million pound integration. Mid private equity buyout, that call sat with executive leadership and would be shaped by far more than design. My job wasn’t to win it, but to make sure they understood what each option meant for customers.

They backed the full experience, then later confirmed it wasn’t commercially possible. That moved the problem to checkout, where customers would first learn their basket wasn’t fully available once they’d committed to it and were focused on paying. The challenge became how much to tell customers, and how far to encourage them to explore collection options, without derailing them from paying.

How hard should checkout push collection options?

I explored a wide range of ways to handle a part-ready basket, then narrowed them to four directions and ran usability testing on each. They ranged from actively steering customers towards same-day collection to simply asking whether they needed it.

  1. 1. Point to another store.

    If the full order wasn’t ready at their chosen store, checkout pointed to the nearest one that had everything, within a reasonable radius. It was the quickest route to same-day collection. But testing showed stores can be further apart than customers are willing to travel, and most pick theirs because it fits conveniently into their day. Few would switch.

  2. 2. Explain what’s ready, then offer routes in a modal.

    Checkout showed what was ready today and let customers choose: remove the unavailable item and collect today, or switch store. Participants found the choice clear, but the modal interrupted checkout for everyone with a part-ready basket, including those happy to wait.

  3. 3. Show the options on the page.

    The same options sat inline, so customers could choose without leaving the flow. In testing, it made checkout longer and harder to scan, with most participants reading past options they didn’t need.

  4. 4. Lead with the customer’s need.

    This asked “Need to collect your order today?” with the options one click away. Participants preferred the lighter touch, but it hid which item was holding up the order, the one detail they needed to decide whether to wait.

What testing changed.

I knew both mindsets existed, but I’d assumed same-day was the priority. Testing showed it wasn’t. Customers with a genuinely urgent need said they’d walk into a store or a local supermarket rather than order online. For those buying online, collection was about convenience: a store on their way to work or near home, and they were happy to wait. Same-day was a bonus, not a reason to change plans. What mattered was a seamless checkout that told them clearly when their order would be ready, and why, without getting in the way.

That reframed all four directions. Three pushed options most customers would never use. The fourth stayed light but hid the one detail they needed.

The final design.

I designed for the majority without shutting out anyone else. Checkout defaults to the soonest date the full order can be collected, since that’s what most customers would choose anyway. It names the item holding the order up, so customers can judge at a glance whether waiting is fine. And it asks for nothing. The message can be dismissed, and anyone who needs something today can still change store or edit their basket.

It was the right answer for Boots too. Two directions invited customers to remove items, meaning smaller orders, and every extra decision at checkout is another reason to drop off. The final design keeps the basket whole and checkout moving, while being honest about a service Boots wants customers to trust.

03

Rethinking the journey after payment, at the scale of more than 100 million transactional emails a year

The journey after payment.

Every Boots order is followed by a confirmation page and a series of emails that keep the customer updated until it arrives. Across all its customers, Boots sends more than 100 million of these emails a year. Yet each one read as if it had been written by a different person. There was no content strategy, the visual hierarchy buried what mattered, and the handoffs to things like returns were weak.

The team had planned a refresh. I made the case for treating every message after payment as one connected journey, and then took ownership of it.

Designing every scenario.

I designed every email and wrote the copy across more than a hundred of them, covering every path an order can take. The complexity came from what an order can contain. Retail, pharmacy, dropship and marketplace items each have their own rules for delivery and returns, and their own loyalty details, and a single order can mix all four.

For each stage, I decided what the customer needed to know, what should carry over from the last message, and what was new. This mattered most when an order arrived in parts, with dropship items sent by different suppliers on different days. Each email had to make clear what was coming, from whom and when, without contradicting the one before it.

Bringing marketing with me.

The emails had always belonged to marketing, so this meant rethinking their work. Rather than leading with what was wrong, I focused on what a connected journey would give them: clearer messages, fewer confused customers and a better relationship after every order.

The hardest conversation was about advertising. Marketing wanted ads in the confirmation email. I pushed back, because that email has one job: reassuring someone their order went through and telling them what happens next. Selling to them there works against it. Instead, I showed marketing better moments to reach customers, and smarter ones too, such as following up once the return window has passed and the customer has clearly kept their order. Marketing kept their reach, and the confirmation email kept its purpose.

A system built to last.

Designing more than a hundred emails one by one would have left them drifting apart again within a year. So I created a modular template system instead. The fundamentals, such as buttons, typography and colour, came from the Boots brand. The templates themselves were built from patterns that could be combined and adapted for any scenario, from a straightforward delivery to an order split across several suppliers.

I brought engineering in early, to understand how Salesforce Marketing Cloud would handle the templates and the conditional logic behind them. The default would have been a separate template for every email. Instead, I made the case for a small set of flexible templates, with content blocks that switch on or off depending on what’s in the order. It meant fewer templates to build and test, and emails that couldn’t drift apart, because they were all assembled from the same pieces.

The emails share their patterns with the confirmation page. The first email arrives moments after a customer lands on it, so the two feel like one continuous experience. The result is a journey after payment that feels as considered as the checkout before it, rather than an afterthought.

The impact so far.

Checkout goes live in phases from Q4 2026, as part of a multi-year, multi-million pound Salesforce transformation across Boots, so there’s no live conversion figure yet. But the work has already had an effect.

The new checkout structure has been proven, beating the existing one in a live pilot. The patterns I designed are already being used beyond checkout, across Boots.com. And every change has been agreed with the teams who own it, from payments and loyalty to marketing and legal, so what goes live has the business behind it as well as the evidence.
