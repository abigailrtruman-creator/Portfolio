Overview

The brief started with introducing Same-Day Click & Collect into Boots’ fulfilment offering. As lead product designer, I made the case to use that moment to rethink every point where fulfilment choices shape the customer experience, from the product page to checkout and post-purchase communications.

I led the design work and process end to end, working closely with product, engineering and teams across payments, loyalty, personalisation, marketing and legal, while navigating significant technology constraints along the way. The work was structured around three connected pieces:

01

Proving the case for change in a checkout that already converted

I began with research to establish what customers actually needed before changing an already high-converting checkout. Years of optimisation had produced a single-page flow presented as five steps, with a progress bar that did not reflect the real experience. It had improved conversion when it launched, so identifying the inconsistency alone was not enough to justify a redesign.

Initial research found no clear preference for a progress bar, or for a particular number of steps. Customers were consistent about the underlying need: checkout should feel quick, ask only for essential information and communicate clearly. That meant moving beyond opinions about the component and testing two routes: one made the best use of a properly designed progress bar, while the other explored a clearer way to signal progress in a single-page checkout. I A/B tested both to establish the stronger experience:

Test AMulti‑step checkout
A progress bar and a separate page for each step, matching the stepped journey the bar implied.
Test BSingle‑page checkout
Numbered sections, revealed progressively and collapsed into editable summaries once complete.

InsightA single page made checkout feel faster and more controllable.

Most participants preferred Test B because they could see the full journey from the start. This made the amount of information feel manageable, showed what was still to come, and made it easy to review or change an answer. Numbered sections gave customers a clear sense of progress without implying separate pages.

User preference alone was not enough. An already high-converting checkout needed evidence of commercial impact, so I partnered with Boots’ optimisation team to A/B test the single-page design against the live checkout.

+0.78%

Higher conversion than the live checkout.

With more than one million sessions on each version, the uplift was statistically significant and translated into thousands of additional orders during the test.

The result was large enough to rule out chance and settled the case for change. It also gave me a proven foundation for bringing the new Same-Day Click & Collect experience into checkout.

Beyond the structure, working through the detail with each owner.

The progress bar wasn’t the only thing that needed to change. Other parts of the existing checkout weren’t right either, and I could see it. But seeing it wasn’t enough. Each of those areas was owned by a team with business reasons for how it worked, from commercial partnerships to loyalty strategy. Showing that customers preferred a change counts for little if the business can’t accept it. So I worked through each one with its owner first, and only took a direction into testing once they’d approved it.

Payment methods.
Data showed which methods customers used most, but they had to stay in the order each provider joined Boots, a commercial agreement. So I kept the order and changed the presentation. Every method now sits in one view with equal weight, and choosing card defaults to a saved card, keeping checkout quick while giving every provider equal footing.
Advantage Card points.
Paying with points was a small grey link in a corner, and customers only found out they were short by hovering over it. I moved points into the main payment list with the balance up front. On mobile and tablet, I also brought the order total and points earned out of a collapsed accordion, so customers saw both at the moment they paid.
Marketing consent.
The smallest change took the most convincing. The checkbox sat in the first step, with long and sometimes inaccurate wording. Marketing were understandably risk-averse, so I took them through it line by line: what each phrase was meant to do versus what customers took from it. Once legal signed off new language, marketing agreed to clearer wording and a move to sit beside the terms at payment, where customers expect to check what they’re agreeing to.

Getting each direction approved first meant the usability testing that followed was a much better use of time. It wasn’t spent testing ideas the business might reject. With the owners already behind each change, I led the testing to refine the experience for customers. By the time these changes reached the build, they had the backing of the business and evidence that they made a better experience for customers.

02

Introducing Same-Day Click & Collect, despite big technical limits

Starting with what we knew we could promise.

The technology was still undecided, but each product page would be able to show two things. Whether an item was in stock in-store, and whether it could be collected. Customers needed to tell those apart, because collection depended on whether the store could meet its cut-off that day.

I designed the delivery and Click & Collect module on the product page, and the store selection modal behind it. I tested several wording variations with customers, and the phrasing shown here came out on top. It made the difference between in-store stock and collection clear at a glance.

Making sure leadership chose with the full picture.

A promise for the whole basket was harder. Retail, pharmacy, dropship and marketplace items each behave differently, and one basket can mix them. Someone collecting medicine might need it today, while someone buying shampoo would happily wait. Delivering that promise needed a multi-million pound integration, in the middle of a private equity buyout. That was always going to be a hard case to make. My job was to make sure executive leadership understood what each option would cost customers, and where that might show up commercially.

After weighing the investment, leadership chose technology that checks availability item by item. A promise for the whole basket would only be known at checkout, once customers had committed and were focused on paying. The challenge became how much to tell them, and how far to encourage them to explore collection, without derailing them from paying.

How hard should checkout push collection options?

I explored a wide range of ways to handle a part-ready basket, then narrowed them to four directions and ran usability testing on each. They ranged from actively steering customers towards same-day collection to simply asking whether they needed it.

  1. 1. Point to another store.

    If the full order wasn’t ready at their chosen store, checkout pointed to the nearest one that had everything, within a reasonable radius. It was the quickest route to same-day collection. Testing showed stores can be further apart than customers are willing to travel, and most pick theirs because it fits conveniently into their day.

  2. 2. Explain what’s ready, then offer routes in a modal.

    Checkout showed what was ready today and let customers choose: remove the unavailable item and collect today, or switch store. Participants found the choice clear. The modal still stopped checkout for everyone with a part-ready basket.

  3. 3. Show the options on the page.

    The same options sat inline, so customers could choose without leaving the flow. Most participants read past options they didn’t need.

  4. 4. Lead with the customer’s need.

    This asked “Need to collect your order today?” with the options one click away. Participants preferred the lighter touch. The item holding the order up, the one detail they needed to decide whether to wait, stayed out of sight.

Insight

Three of the four directions asked customers to change store. In testing, most participants wouldn’t.

The usual store beats a faster one

Their regular Boots sat on the commute or near home, and the nearest alternative was too far to stay convenient. Many saw same-day as a bonus, not a reason to change plans. If something was urgent and unavailable, they’d get it from a supermarket rather than rebuild the order.

Knowing what’s holding the order up is enough

Some participants wanted to see which item was causing the delay, so they could decide what to do. They make that call on other retail sites all the time and didn’t need checkout to guide them.

The agreed solution.

The final design kept the lighter touch participants preferred and put the cause of the delay back on the page.

  • The soonest date is selected by default. Most customers would pick it anyway, so the common path needs no extra step.

  • A message names the item holding the order up. Customers can judge whether waiting works for them without being asked to decide.

  • Changing course stays optional. The message can be dismissed, and anyone who needs it today can still change store or edit the basket.

  • The basket stays whole. Removing the item would have reduced the order value and added a decision at the point of payment.

The design keeps most customers moving through checkout with their full basket. Those who need something today still have a way to get it.

03

Rethinking 100 million post-purchase emails a year

The experience after payment.

Every Boots order is followed by a confirmation page and a series of emails that keep the customer updated until it arrives. Across all its customers, Boots sends more than 100 million of these emails a year. Yet each one read as if it had been written by a different person. There was no content strategy, the visual hierarchy buried what mattered, and the handoffs to things like returns were weak.

The team had planned a refresh. I made the case for treating every message after payment as one connected journey, and then took ownership of it.

Designing every scenario.

I designed every email and wrote the copy across more than a hundred of them, covering every path an order can take. The complexity came from what an order can contain. Retail, pharmacy, dropship and marketplace items each have their own rules for delivery and returns, and their own loyalty details, and a single order can mix all four.

For each stage, I decided what the customer needed to know, what should carry over from the last message, and what was new. This mattered most when an order arrived in parts, with dropship items sent by different suppliers on different days. Each email had to make clear what was coming, from whom and when, without contradicting the one before it.

Protecting transactional emails from becoming marketing material.

To marketing, order emails were a channel. To customers, they were part of the service, the main way of getting key updates about their order. Marketing had always owned these emails, so changing them meant getting their buy-in. Rather than telling them what was wrong, I framed the change around what they’d gain: customer trust and, with it, retention.

Marketing also had a clear agenda for upselling and cross-selling in these emails. I was confident that wasn’t right for customers, so I ran usability testing to understand what they expected from each email and what mattered most. None of the participants expected marketing content in their transactional emails. With that evidence, we agreed to move it later in the journey, to after the dispatch email, at a point where it’s more likely to convert.

One system for more than a hundred emails.

Designing more than a hundred emails one by one would have left them drifting apart again within a year. Instead, I created a modular template system. The fundamentals came from the Boots brand, and the templates were built from patterns that combine for any scenario, from a straightforward delivery to an order split across several suppliers.

I brought engineering in early to understand how Salesforce Marketing Cloud would handle the templates and their conditional logic. The default would have been a separate template for every email. I made the case for a small set of flexible templates, with content blocks that switch on or off depending on what’s in the order. That meant fewer templates to build and test, and emails that stay consistent because they share the same pieces.

The emails also share patterns with the confirmation page. The first one arrives moments after a customer lands there, so the experience after payment feels as considered as the checkout before it.

The impact so far.

Checkout launches in phases from Q4 2026, as part of a multi-year, multi-million pound Salesforce transformation across Boots. It goes in with the evidence and the business behind it.

  • Proven with live customers

    The new structure increased conversion against the existing checkout in a live optimisation test.

  • Agreed across the business

    Payments and fraud, loyalty and personalisation, marketing and legal all signed off the approach.

  • Adopted beyond checkout

    Its components now sit in the Boots design system, in use across the wider Boots.com redesign.

The full rollout will show the effect at scale. It goes live with confidence, on a tested foundation with the flexibility to grow with the proposition.
