Case study · British Cycling app

Creating British Cycling x Lloyds’ habit-forming cycling app, launched in record time by embracing AI throughout.

  • UX & UI
  • AI Development
  • Strategy
Overview

As design lead, I led the team that took Sofa to Saddle from a blank page to launch in 12 weeks, a habit-forming cycling app built as a joint proposition between Lloyds Bank and British Cycling, with huge ambitions to get 1 million more people cycling by 2028. The scope ran across brand identity, UX, UI and copy, and included briefing external agencies on video and imagery, working with British Cycling’s leadership team, and running a parallel experiment using AI to build the app’s front-end code.

On the surface, this was an app build. Underneath, it was two brands who understood cycling differently. One lived and breathed it. One backed it commercially. That meant two different ideas of what the product was for: is it there to build genuine cycling confidence, or to drive engagement with a banking product. Every decision had to hold that tension without a single customer ever noticing it.

01 / 10 State

A blank page, and a deadline that didn’t care.

There was no existing app to modernise, no legacy patterns, no accumulated user data.

What made it hard wasn’t the idea, it was the timeframe. We had a fraction of the time a build like this usually gets, and a budget to match.

And it wasn’t one app. The contract committed us to two — Sofa to Saddle, and a second app for children — with the same functionality underneath both. Every requirement came out of workshops with Lloyds and British Cycling in the room together, and nothing was settled until both had put their input in and both had signed it off. So before designing anything I mapped the two propositions stage by stage to find where they genuinely overlapped and where they had to diverge — which is what told us how to structure the app, and what twelve weeks and this budget could actually buy.

02 / 10 Insight

Getting back on a bike isn’t a fitness problem. It’s a confidence one.

Cycling has an intimidation problem: lycra, jargon, the sense that everyone else already knows what they’re doing. The people we were designing for weren’t always unmotivated, they were unsure where to start and scared of getting it wrong in public.

It’s the same insight Couch to 5k built on for running, and it became the spine of Sofa to Saddle.

03 / 10 Principle

Meet people at their actual level, not the level the sport assumes.

Every plan starts from where the user actually is, not where a cyclist is assumed to be. Competency-based plans, levels that unlock as you go, badges that reward consistency over performance.

None of that is a technically difficult pattern. What mattered was resisting the instinct to make it look more sophisticated than it needed to be.

04 / 10 Craft

Confidence from one, rhythm from the other.

Two brands that had appeared together before, but never had to speak with one voice. Lloyds brought trust and restraint. British Cycling brought energy and speed. Neither language was built to sit next to the other, and a straight compromise would have satisfied nobody.

We built an identity that borrowed confidence from Lloyds and rhythm from British Cycling, without trying to look exactly like either parent brand. It wasn’t an easy sign-off, but it was the version both sides could actually stand behind.

05 / 10 Leadership

Two sets of objectives, one call to make.

Good judgement meant knowing both brands’ objectives well enough to decide, case by case, whose priority mattered most and why. Every call still had to earn its keep commercially: good enough to ship, never gold-plated past scope or budget.

I held the strategy across every layer of the product, down to the smallest interaction, reviewing the detail and correcting it where it hadn’t been considered enough.

The product map is where a lot of that got done. I mapped the whole flow and we walked it screen by screen with both clients in the room, looking for the gaps — not gaps in the screens, gaps in the proposition. The sharpest one: the app exists to get a nervous beginner out on a first ride, and British Cycling’s own offer, insurance and membership, is built for riders who already are one. Getting someone from finishing a plan to a real relationship with British Cycling was never an interaction we could design our way out of, and the map is what put it on the table early enough to be a strategy conversation instead of a launch problem.

06 / 10 System

A progress system built once, used everywhere.

The level and badge system was designed as a single reusable component rather than one-off screens for each milestone. Whichever plan a user is on, the same progress logic and badge structure applies underneath. That meant we could add new plans later without redesigning the core experience each time.

07 / 10 Craft

A voice that encourages without patronising.

Habit-forming apps live or die on tone. I defined the principles and worked with a copywriter to develop it alongside the UI, not after it, so tone of voice and interaction design grew together instead of one dictating to the other.

The voice we landed on was matter-of-fact rather than relentlessly upbeat, acknowledging effort without treating every ride like a triumph.

08 / 10 System

Two front ends, built in parallel.

Alongside the development team building the app’s front end, I ran a parallel build: could a designer produce a production-quality front end using AI, matching an experienced engineer on quality and security. Before writing a line of code, we codified our coding standards into rules for the AI to follow, holding the output to the same bar an engineer would hold themselves to.

The two front ends came out equally solid. That result has changed how Elixirr thinks about the line between design and engineering, and how we sell and structure work with clients since.

09 / 10 Honest bit

A deliberate bet on speed over scope.

The budget didn’t stretch to anything experimental, so version one shipped as a focused MVP: level plans, badges, progress tracking. That was a choice, not a limitation we settled for.

The ambition went into speed and coherence, not feature breadth, because the app wasn’t working alone. Joe Marler fronted the campaign, it made the news, and the real goal was getting people talking about cycling. The digital experience was there to support that, not carry it alone.

10 / 10 Outcome

Live, and still just the beginning.

3,000 sign-ups on day one, and growing. Sofa to Saddle is live, built by a small team in a fraction of the usual timeframe, and counting towards 1 million more people cycling by 2028.

Beyond the app itself, the AI front-end experiment reshaped how Elixirr pitches and staffs projects, and the badge and progress system is built to carry whatever comes next.

Reflection

What this one changed about how I work.

Two things I’m taking forward.

The first isn’t really about AI. The parallel front end worked because we wrote the standards down before we wrote any code and held the output to them, which is the same discipline that keeps a design system from drifting. The result settled an argument that has outlasted the project — how Elixirr sells this kind of work, and who it puts on it.

The second is the one I’d change. Twelve weeks bought a launch, and a launch is not the same as proof. Version one shipped with the levels, the badges and the tracking, which is enough to start a habit and not enough to know whether one formed. I’d want the next twelve pointed at that: not more features, but evidence that the confidence this app is built to give people lasts past the first plan.
