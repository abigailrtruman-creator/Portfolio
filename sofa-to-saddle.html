Creating British Cycling x Lloyds’ habit-forming cycling app, launched in record time by embracing AI throughout.

Overview

Research showed that for most people, cycling isn’t a fitness problem. It’s a confidence one. The average Briton spends 18 hours a week on the sofa and 70% say they should exercise more, but most don’t know where to start.

British Cycling saw an opportunity there. As part of its goal to get a million more people cycling by 2028, it partnered with Lloyds Bank to launch Sofa to Saddle, an app to help people build the habit. It had 12 weeks, a fraction of the time and budget a build like this usually gets.

As design lead, I was hands on from the first sketch to the last line of front end code. I designed most of the UX and UI and built the entire front end in Cursor. Alongside that, I led a small team of content writers and another designer, and steered both client organisations through the product strategy.

Doing it in 12 weeks meant getting three things right:

01

Turning a feature list into a strategy-led product.

The brief arrived as a list of features, with no clear sense of how the app would build a habit or what success looked like long term. With two organisations and around 20 stakeholders, I needed to guide both clients to a shared proposition: should the app keep people in, or help them build the habit and move on?

01

Turning a feature list into a strategy-led product.

The brief was a list of features. Each one made sense on its own, but there was no product strategy behind them, and no clear idea of the value the product should create.

A first version made that gap visible. Mapped out as journeys and mid-fidelity wireframes, it gave both clients something concrete to react to.

I then ran a workshop with around 20 senior stakeholders from Lloyds Bank and British Cycling. It surfaced the decisions that would shape the product. It also showed that most of them were being framed around the app, such as how to keep people progressing and coming back.

Changing the questions we were answering.

I got both client teams to reframe each decision around the rider instead, and what would help someone build a cycling habit that supports their own goal, whatever that might be. One insight ran through every answer. Getting back on a bike is a confidence problem before it’s a fitness one.

For each decision, I talked both teams through the options and trade-offs. Where a call needed evidence, I tested it with new riders before making a recommendation.

01

Plan structure

Every training app needs a way for riders to progress. The question was what that progress should lead to.

They asked

How do we keep riders in the app?

I asked

When does someone stop needing us?

We talked through three ways to structure the plan. Levels based on competency gave a clear sense of progress, but focused on how good someone was rather than whether they kept riding. A series of plans with different objectives offered variety, but no natural end. A single plan, matched to each rider’s starting point, ended in graduation.

I made the case for the single plan as the best fit for people building a new habit. A clear finish feels manageable and keeps the focus on turning up. Graduation also gives the app a natural moment to point riders to their next step, whether that’s with British Cycling or somewhere else. Riders can still adjust the plan if it feels too hard or too easy, and in testing, the finish line made the goal feel attainable.

02

Personalisation

Every rider takes a short quiz before their plan starts. The question was what the plan should change in response.

They asked

How do we match each plan to how fit someone is?

I asked

What made them want to ride in the first place?

Personalising on fitness alone was the simplest to build, but treated everyone at the same level the same way. A separate plan for each goal would fit people closely, but meant building far more rides. Our research showed people ride for very different reasons, so I steered the clients towards a middle path. Rides stay matched to ability, while the content around them changes in tone of voice and context to fit each rider’s reason for riding. A content strategist and a sports psychologist wrote each version, and I shaped the structure and reviewed every one. In testing, riders expected the plan to reflect why they were riding.

03

Ride tracking

Riders need a way to record each ride. The question was what that record should show.

They asked

Can we plug in Strava?

I asked

How should someone feel when they finish their first ride?

We compared three ways to track a ride. Strava was credible, but built around performance, and would take beginners out of our app at their least confident. Native phone tracking kept them with us, but risked turning each ride into numbers to fall short on. A simple timer meant giving up distance and route data. Most rides in the plan were time-based, so I recommended the timer. It works on any bike, including static and borrowed ones, and distance stays optional for riders who want it.

04

Engagement

A habit needs something to bring riders back between rides. The question was how much the app should do.

They asked

How do we make sure nobody forgets about us?

I asked

What makes someone want to ride again next week?

More touchpoints might lift engagement early, but risked overwhelming beginners. Badges for every event could stack until they meant nothing, and frequent nudges tend to get switched off. Fewer moments meant fewer chances to bring someone back, so each one had to count. I steered the clients towards that approach. Content unlocks in small steps, badges mark real milestones, and riders self-report their rides to keep the focus on turning up. I cut the proposed notifications to five, each placed where a nudge was most likely to help.

02

Building the components and flows that brought the brand to life.

An agency had created the brand guidelines: bold blue and green, condensed type, capsule shapes and a fast-forward motif. They set the tone, but they weren’t built for a product, and there was no design system behind them.

Working at pace with one other designer, I owned the moments that would make or break a nervous beginner’s confidence, along with the system underneath that let us both move quickly. I reviewed the rest as it developed, leaving comments in Figma and then talking them through in a 1-1, showing how I’d have approached it. The aim wasn’t to take the work off them, but to help them see the problem differently, so the whole app held the same standard.

Onboarding: momentum from the first tap.

Sign-up is where a hesitant rider is most likely to give up, so it had to feel like progress, not admin. One question per screen keeps each step light. Each screen changes colour, the capsules frame photography of real riders, and the chevrons push people forward, so filling in a form feels like momentum. The brand’s “switch on” toggle marks the moment sign-up is complete.

The quiz and plan: making the first step look small.

The quiz is deliberately plain, so the questions do the work. Not every rider takes it straight away, so I designed the screens they’d land on instead. Home and the plan tab both point to the same “Get started” button. On home, the ask is short: four questions, under two minutes. The plan tab is where someone might wonder if this is a proper plan, so it says who built it before asking for anything. The photography shows everyday riders rather than athletes, because someone starting out needs to see themselves in it.

Once the quiz is done, each level shows what it’s for and how long it takes. Ride checklists and bike care videos sit on the plan itself, since that’s where beginners look first.

Badges: rewards that mean something.

The guidelines defined badges as abstract geometric shapes that looked striking but didn’t tell riders what they’d achieved. I kept the geometric language and gave every badge a meaning you could read at a glance: a sofa for getting off it, a bike for the first ride, a heart for reflecting on how a ride felt. Because event-driven badges could stack two or three in a single ride, each was reshaped into a distinct milestone. Every badge uses the same template, so new ones can be added without redesigning anything.

03

Can a designer ship a production-quality front end?

Alongside the development team, I ran a parallel build in Cursor to answer one question: could a designer produce a production-quality front end with AI, matching an experienced engineer on quality and security? Speed only mattered if the result could be trusted. So the rules came before the code, and an engineer reviewed the result independently.

AI-built front ends are usually treated as prototypes, quick to make and thrown away before production. My view was that this didn’t have to be true. The gap between a design and the shipped app is where most experiences lose their quality. If a designer could build to an engineer’s standard, that gap closes, and the experience can keep improving as it’s built.

Making trust the first thing I built.

The risk with AI isn’t speed. It’s that it can be confidently inconsistent. So before any screen existed, I worked with one of the developers to write our engineering standard down, then made the tooling enforce it, so quality never depended on me remembering.

  • 26 rules in the repo before a line of app code, with the styling rules kept swappable so one command could repoint the whole standard.
  • Six hooks that blocked destructive commands, kept Figma read-only, and ran the type checker and tests the moment the AI stopped.
  • Three review agents checking the work as it went, including a security reviewer working to OWASP’s mobile standard.

Building something engineers would want to inherit.

A designer’s build only has value if engineers would happily take it over. So I matched their world rather than inventing my own.

  • The same React Native setup as the development team, so any of them could pick it up.
  • One small set of shared components, with every colour, type size and spacing value in a single file. A Figma change lands in one place, not forty.
  • Real sign-in on Firebase from day one, with the signed-in check at the app’s entrance so no new screen could be left unprotected.

Usable before the backend existed.

Usually, nobody can use the app until the backend lands, and by then changing the experience is expensive. Here, the whole journey could be used from day one.

  • Sign-up, the quiz, eight levels of plan content, badges and their unlock rules, and the post-ride mood capture, all running on stand-in data.
  • Wired as three separate switches, so each area moved to real data as it arrived, with no single risky changeover.

What being in the code made possible.

Figma shows how an app should look. A running build shows how it behaves, and I could change it there. An engineer only came in where a change genuinely needed one.

  • Layouts that broke on a smaller phone were reworked in the build, moving elements or adding a screen where the flow needed it.
  • Motion and the smaller interactions were tuned by using the app, not described in a handover.
  • Error and empty states were tried with real data, so problems showed up before QA.
  • Testing on real phones caught clipped type, an off-centre avatar initial, a misplaced heading and screens that redrew badly after a ride.
  • On iOS the display font clipped the tops of its capitals. It was fixed once in the shared components, then written into the rules so it couldn’t come back.

Holding it to the same bar as engineering.

Matching an engineer meant matching their scrutiny, not just their output.

  • Every screen rebuilt against the Figma frames themselves, read from the file rather than measured off a screenshot.
  • A security review against OWASP’s mobile standard found two issues worth fixing. A password-reset code was being passed in a web address, where it could be logged and reused. And a default setting could have quietly fallen back to stand-in data in the live app. Both were fixed before release.
  • Learning content moved into a content management system, so British Cycling can change the words without an app release.

An independent engineer reviewed both front ends, and they came out equally solid on quality and security. The difference was that mine had been refined throughout, because the designer could iterate on it as it was built. The point was never to replace the development team. It was to show that design and engineering can run in parallel, with the designer owning the experience right through to the code.

The impact so far.

Sofa to Saddle went live in twelve weeks, and it’s already doing its job.

  • 3,000 riders on day one

    Sign-ups landed on launch day, with strong early feedback. Every one counts towards British Cycling’s goal of a million more people cycling by 2028.

  • A new way Elixirr delivers

    I proved a designer can own the front end without the quality slipping. The two builds came out equally solid on quality and security, and Elixirr now pitches and staffs projects that way.

  • British Cycling asked for me back

    On the strength of Sofa to Saddle and the relationships built along the way, British Cycling asked for me to lead the redesign of their clubs and events platform.
