Case study · British Cycling app

Creating British Cycling x Lloyds’ habit-forming cycling app, launched in record time by embracing AI throughout.

  • UX & UI
  • AI Development
  • Strategy
Overview

As design lead, I led a team of designers, content writers, strategists and consultants from a blank page to launch in 12 weeks. Sofa to Saddle is a cycling app for Lloyds Bank and British Cycling.

I also built a parallel front end in Cursor. First time Elixirr had seen a designer code an app properly. We wrote the practice down. It’s how this work gets staffed and sold now.

On the surface, this was an app build. Underneath, a way back onto a bike.

01 / 10 State

A blank page, and a deadline that didn’t care.

There was no existing app to modernise, no legacy patterns, no accumulated user data.

What made it hard wasn’t the idea, it was the timeframe. We had a fraction of the time a build like this usually gets, and a budget to match. I was leading designers, content writers, strategists and consultants into that. Twelve weeks only works if people can keep moving. I wasn’t going to spend it holding court over a Figma file.

And it wasn’t one app. The contract committed us to two: Sofa to Saddle, and a second app for children, with the same functionality underneath both. Every requirement came out of workshops with Lloyds and British Cycling in the room together, and nothing was settled until both had put their input in and both had signed it off. So before designing anything I mapped the two propositions stage by stage to find where they genuinely overlapped and where they had to diverge. That told us how to structure the app, and what twelve weeks and this budget could actually buy.

02 / 10 Insight

Getting back on a bike isn’t a fitness problem. It’s a confidence one.

This was the insight that fuelled the ambition. Cycling has an intimidation problem: lycra, jargon, the sense that everyone else already knows what they’re doing. Some people simply weren’t motivated. A lot more already knew they should exercise, and wanted to. They just needed a helping hand. British Cycling’s research put numbers to that: over 18 hours of sofa time a week, and 70% who think they should exercise more.

What riding gives back is almost none of it fitness. It is how a ride leaves people feeling: clearer-headed, calmer, better about themselves. So the aim of the app was a confidence one: a free plan pointed at the hurdles rather than at fitness, the same insight Couch to 5k built on for running.

03 / 10 Principle

Meet people at their actual level, not the level the sport assumes.

Every plan starts from where the user actually is, not where a cyclist is assumed to be. Competency-based plans, levels that unlock as you go, badges that reward consistency over performance.

Alongside the rides, the eight weeks carry the things a nervous beginner actually needs and a training plan usually leaves out: milestone rewards, basic bike care, and step-by-step route support from health professionals and Great Britain Cycling Team specialists. Each of those is there to answer something the research turned up rather than to add a feature.

None of that is a technically difficult pattern. What mattered was resisting the instinct to make it look more sophisticated than it needed to be.

04 / 10 Craft

Confidence from one, rhythm from the other.

Two brands that had appeared together before, but never had to speak with one voice. Lloyds brought trust and restraint. British Cycling brought energy and speed. Neither language was built to sit next to the other, and a straight compromise would have satisfied nobody.

We built an identity that borrowed confidence from Lloyds and rhythm from British Cycling, without trying to look exactly like either parent brand. It wasn’t an easy sign-off, but it was the version both sides could actually stand behind.

05 / 10 Leadership

Two sets of objectives, one call to make.

Good judgement meant knowing both brands’ objectives well enough to decide, case by case, whose priority mattered most and why. Every call still had to earn its keep commercially: good enough to ship, never gold-plated past scope or budget.

The designers got the same standard. I review work offline, leave comments in Figma, then we talk it through in a 1-1. I’ll show how I would have gone at it. Not to take the work off them. So they leave seeing the problem differently.

The product map is where a lot of the strategy got done. I mapped the whole flow and we walked it screen by screen with both clients in the room, looking for the gaps. Not in the screens. In the proposition. The sharpest one: the app exists to get a nervous beginner out on a first ride, and British Cycling’s own offer, insurance and membership, is built for riders who already are one. Getting someone from finishing a plan to a real relationship with British Cycling was never an interaction we could design our way out of, and the map is what put it on the table early enough to be a strategy conversation instead of a launch problem.

06 / 10 System

A progress system built once, used everywhere.

The level and badge system was designed as a single reusable component rather than one-off screens for each milestone. Whichever plan a user is on, the same progress logic and badge structure applies underneath. That meant we could add new plans later without redesigning the core experience each time.

07 / 10 Craft

A voice that encourages without patronising.

Habit-forming apps live or die on tone. I defined the principles and worked with a copywriter to develop it alongside the UI, not after it, so tone of voice and interaction design grew together instead of one dictating to the other. Same as with the designers. Comments in the file, then a conversation.

The voice we landed on was matter-of-fact rather than relentlessly upbeat, acknowledging effort without treating every ride like a triumph.

08 / 10 Experiment

Could a designer build the front end? I used Cursor to find out.

Alongside the development team building the app’s front end, I ran a parallel build: could a designer produce a production-quality front end using AI, matching an experienced engineer on quality and security. Before writing a line of code, we wrote our coding standards into rules for the AI, and held the output to the same bar an engineer would hold themselves to.

I was in the code, so engineers came in for the pieces that needed them. Interaction and motion problems showed up in the build, not as a QA ticket two weeks later. The two front ends came out equally solid. First time Elixirr had proved it. We wrote the practice down for the next client team. That’s how this work gets sold and staffed now.

Agreeing the rules before writing any code

  • 26 rules in the repo before a line of app code: file structure, naming, how a screen is composed, how a component is built, what has to be tested.
  • Five were the styling rules, kept swappable, so one command could repoint the whole standard at a different styling approach.
  • 13 skills for the jobs that recurred, five saved commands, and three review agents checking the work as it went. One was a security reviewer working to OWASP’s mobile standard.
  • Six hooks enforced it instead of my memory: destructive commands blocked, writes back to Figma blocked so designs could be read but never overwritten, and the type checker and tests run the moment the AI stopped.
  • Jira and Figma connected directly, so a ticket and the frame it referred to were read from the source.

The foundations, and a real sign-in

  • Built on the same standard React Native setup as the development team, so any of them could pick it up.
  • Every screen built from one small set of shared components, with every colour, type size and spacing value in a single file. A Figma change lands in one place, not forty.
  • Sign-up, sign-in, password reset and email verification all run on Firebase, so passwords and sessions are handled by a service built for it.
  • The signed-in check sits at the entrance to the app, not on each screen, so a new screen can’t be left unprotected.

The screens, built before the data existed

  • Onboarding, a ten-screen sign-up, and the six-question quiz that picks a rider’s plan: their goal, how much they exercise, their last ride, whether they can get a bike.
  • Then the plans: eight levels of content, progress tracking, badges and the rules that unlock them, the achievements grid, and the mood capture after a ride.
  • All of it ran on stand-in data while the backend was still being built, so the whole app could be clicked through from day one.
  • Wired as three separate switches, so each part moved to real data as it arrived. No single risky changeover.

Checked against the designs, then against security

  • Every screen rebuilt against the Figma frames themselves, read out of the file rather than measured off a screenshot.
  • Brand fonts loading properly, and the shapes Figma draws by masking rebuilt so they hold their edges at any size.
  • One iOS bug kept returning: the display font clipped the tops of its capitals. Fixed inside the shared components, then written into the rules.
  • Then a security review against OWASP’s mobile standard. Two things worth fixing: a password-reset code travelling in a web address, and a default that could have fallen back to fake data.

Real data, and the account screens

  • Stand-in data swapped for the real backend one area at a time: quiz, sign-up, profile, plans, activity. Then deleted outright rather than left switched off.
  • Profile screens reuse the sign-up screens with the progress bar and Next button taken out, rather than a second copy of the same form.
  • Changing a password and deleting an account both ask the rider to sign in again first.
  • Learning content and FAQs moved into a content management system, so British Cycling can change the words without an app release.

Polish, on real phones

  • Fixes driven by testing on real handsets rather than a simulator: clipped type, an off-centre avatar initial, a misplaced heading, screens redrawing badly after a ride.
  • Every bug that turned up twice was written down with its fix, so nothing was worked out twice.
09 / 10 Honest bit

A deliberate bet on speed over scope.

The budget didn’t stretch to anything experimental, so version one shipped as a focused MVP: level plans, badges, progress tracking. That was a choice, not a limitation we settled for.

The ambition went into speed and coherence, not feature breadth, because the app wasn’t working alone. Joe Marler fronted the campaign, it made the news, and the real goal was getting people talking about cycling. The digital experience was there to support that, not carry it alone.

10 / 10 Outcome

Live, and still just the beginning.

3,000 sign-ups on day one, and growing. Sofa to Saddle is live, in a fraction of the usual time, by the team I led. It counts towards 1 million more people cycling by 2028.

That target is a share of something already moving. British Cycling counted 30 million people in the UK who cycled at least once in the past year, four million more than in 2022, and cycling here is reckoned to save the NHS £72.7 million. A million more riders is a public health number before it is a product one. Worth remembering, given the app is an eight-week plan and a set of badges.

The other thing that left with us: a designer can own the front end, and pull engineers in for the bits that need them. Elixirr pitches and staffs that way now. The badge and progress system is built to take whatever comes next.

Reflection

What this one changed about how I work.

Two things I’m taking forward.

When the constraints are this stacked, you have to say out loud what done actually is. Twelve weeks, two clients, two apps, a budget that would not take the original scope. Leave that picture standing and the team is already behind. So we named a smaller success: ship a coherent first version under the campaign, with analytics on the levels so we would know if people were progressing. Not more features. A definition the team could hit.

The other one is the AI. People will not follow you into something they do not understand if the only argument is speed. They need to know it is safe. I brought an engineer in to check the code and write a report, so the claim had backing that was not mine. Rules in the repo before a line of app code. The same bar an engineer would hold. Comfort is evidence, not a demo.
